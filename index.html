5.1Modals of Ability, Possibility & Permission

+

Forms at a Glance

InfinitivePresent simplePast simplePresent perfectFuture simple
—cancould—can
to be able tois able towas / were able tohave / has been able towill be able to
—may / mightcould—may / might
⚡ Key point: Be able to is NOT a modal verb — it works in all tenses, including the infinitive and present perfect, where can is impossible: She has been able to finish the report.

Ability

UseModalExamples
General ability (present)canI can speak three languages. / She can grow crops in any climate.
Potential past ability (not necessarily done)couldWhen I was young, I could sleep 12 hours straight.
Specific achievement at one past momentwas/were able toDespite the heavy rain, she was able to finish the race. ✓
She could finish the race. ✗ (affirmative, specific occasion)

Possibility

Examples

may = stronger probability: It may rain later — the sky looks dark.
might = weaker probability: I might come to the party if I finish my homework in time.
can = something possible by nature or conditions: Frost can destroy an entire olive harvest overnight.
could (past): He could be very unreasonable at times.

Permission

ModalRegisterExample
canInformal / everydayCan I borrow your pen?
couldMore politeCould I ask you a personal question?
mayFormalMay I open the window? — Of course, you may.
❌ Contracted negative of may: The form mayn't does NOT exist in standard English. Use the full form: You may not leave the room. ✓

5.2Could, Manage to, Succeed in, Be able to

+

All four structures relate to past ability or achievement — but they are not interchangeable.

StructureMeaningKey detail
couldGeneral ability in the pastNot necessarily performed at a specific moment.
When I was young, I could run very fast.
manage to + infinitiveAchievement — especially if difficult or unexpectedShe managed to pass her exam despite studying only one week.
succeed in + -ingAchievement with difficulty or significanceAfter months of effort, they succeeded in developing a new crop variety.
was / were able toSpecific successful achievement at one momentDespite the storm, we were able to reach the shelter in time.
💡 Follow-up form:
Succeed in always takes the -ing form: She succeeded in passing the test. ✓
Manage to always takes the infinitive: She managed to pass the test. ✓
She succeeded in pass the test. ✗
Extra Examples

The fire spread quickly, but the farmers managed to save most of the crops.
After three attempts, he finally succeeded in getting his driving licence.
I could see the problem, but I wasn't able to fix it on my own.
They didn't succeed in landing a spaceship on the Moon in 1968.

5.3Modals of Deduction

+

Present Tense — Affirmative

ModalCertainty levelExample
must be~95% — very sure it IS trueShe's studied for 10 hours — she must be exhausted.
may / might / could be~30–50% — possible, unsureHe might be hungry — he didn't have breakfast.

Present Tense — Negative

ModalCertainty levelExample
is not100% — a known factShe is not hungry. (I know for certain.)
can't / couldn't be~95% — very sure it is FALSEShe can't be hungry — she just had a full meal!
may not / might not be~30–50% — possibly notHe might not be at home — try calling first.

Past Deduction — must / may / might / could / can't + have + past participle

StructureMeaningExample
must have + ppVery sure it WAS trueShe was late — she must have missed the bus.
may / might have + ppPossibly true in the pastHe might have forgotten about the meeting.
could have + ppOne possible past explanationIt could have been an accident — we're not certain.
can't / couldn't have + ppVery sure it was NOT trueThey can't have eaten already — dinner just started!
🔍 Deduction vs. Obligation: Must for deduction means "I conclude that…" — it is NOT a command. Context makes the difference:
You must be tired after that long journey. → deduction
You must hand in your homework by Friday. → obligation

5.4The Indefinite Article — A / An

+
⚡ A or An? The choice depends on the sound, not the letter.
an hour (h is silent)  |  a university (sounds like "yoo-")  |  an MP (sounds like "em-")  |  a European (sounds like "yoo-")

When to USE A / An

RuleExample
Singular countable noun — first mentionThere is a new student in our class.
The student comes from Sicily.
One of several — a member of a class or typeThere was a cat outside. Have you got a motorbike?
Professions (with be)He's a farmer. She's an engineer. She works as a nurse.
Illnesses (with a / an)I've got a cold / a temperature / a sore throat.
Price, speed, frequency, quantity€2 a kilo / 100 km an hour / twice a week / a lot of
Exclamations before singular countable nounsWhat a lovely day! Such an interesting book!
Parts of the body (if singular)He's got a long nose.

When NOT to Use A / An

RuleCorrect form
Before a possessive adjectivea my friend ✗ → a friend of mine ✓
Before plural nounsa students ✗ → some students ✓
Before uncountable nounsa water ✗ → some water / a glass of water ✓
Some illnesses (no article)I've got measles. He has diabetes.

5.5The Definite Article — The

+

When to USE The

RuleExample
Second mention of a noun already introducedThere's a girl near me. The girl is 17 years old.
Unique things (only one of its kind)the Queen, the government, the sun, the Moon
Superlatives & ordinalsthe best student / the second day / the only thing
Musical instruments (after play)She plays the piano. He plays the violin.
Plural surnames (families)the Simpsons, the Smiths, the Mendels
Rivers, canals, seas, oceans, mountain chains, desertsthe Thames, the Mediterranean, the Alps, the Sahara
Plural islands & countries containing kingdom / republic / statethe Bahamas, the United States, the Netherlands
Adjective used as a noun (group of people)the young, the poor, the elderly, the Italians
Decades and centuriesin the Nineties / in the 21st century
Titles used alone (no name)the Prince, the President

When NOT to Use The

RuleExample
Abstract nouns (general sense)We all admire honesty. Nature can be cruel.
MealsDinner is ready! Let's have breakfast.
Sports and gamesShe plays football. He loves chess.
LanguagesShe speaks Italian and French. I speak Italian.
Colours (general)I like blue.
Proper names with titlesDoctor Marvin, Captain Cook, Uncle Sam, Tom
Singular mountains, lakes, islands, continentsMount Rosa, Lake Como, Sicily, Europe, Africa
Parts of the body preceded by a possessiveShe is washing her hair. He broke his arm.
❌ Tricky pair — instruments vs. sports:
She plays the piano. ✓ (musical instrument → the)
She plays football. ✓ (sport → no article)
She plays the football. ✗
💡 Similar terms, dissimilar use:
listen to the radio / surf the Internet / watch TV (no article before TV)
the Financial Times (newspaper with the) / Time (magazine, no the)
at the seaside (= in the area near the sea) / at sea (= out in the sea)
Unit 6 · Obligation, Comparatives & Modifiers

6.1Modals of Obligation, Necessity & Advice

+
ModalMeaningExample
must / have toSelf-imposed obligation (often 1st person)I must / I have to stop smoking.
mustObligation from the speaker (authority/urgency)Students must hand in their essays by Friday.
have toExternal obligation (rule, law, circumstance)I have to wear a uniform — it's the school rule.
must (strong recommendation)Speaker feels strongly — NOT have toYou must read this book — it's marvellous!
should / ought toAdvice — weaker than mustYou should drink more water every day. Drivers ought to respect speed limits.
mustn'tProhibition (do NOT do this)You mustn't use your phone during the exam.
don't have toNo obligation — free choiceIt's Saturday — you don't have to get up early!
❌ The most common confusion at B2 level:
mustn't = prohibition → forbidden!
don't have to = no obligation → no need, but you can if you want

You mustn't bring your textbook. ✗ (implies it is forbidden)
You don't have to bring your textbook. ✓ (not necessary — your choice)

Modals of Regret — should/ought to + have + past participle

Expresses disapproval or regret about something that happened (or didn't happen) in the past:

Examples

You shouldn't have said that — it was very rude.
I ought to have called you. I'm sorry.
She should have studied harder for the B2 exam.
We shouldn't have stayed out so late — we're exhausted.

6.2Need — Semi-Modal & Regular Verb

+
FormTypeMeaningExample
needn't + base formSemi-modalNot necessary (= don't have to)You needn't worry — everything is fine.
need to + infinitiveRegular verbExpress necessityShe needs to improve her vocabulary before the exam.
don't / doesn't need toRegular verbNo necessityYou don't need to shout — I can hear you perfectly.
needn't have + ppSemi-modalDid something that was unnecessaryThey needn't have met so early — the meeting was postponed.
need + nounRegular verbBefore a noun objectI don't need your help, thanks.
💡 needn't have vs. didn't need to — a crucial B2 distinction:
needn't have + pp → they DID do it, but it turned out to be unnecessary.
didn't need to + inf → it wasn't necessary, so they probably didn't do it.

You needn't have bought flowers — but thank you, it was very kind!
You didn't need to buy flowers, so I didn't bother.

Key Usage Rules

  • Comparatives are followed by than: Organic farming is more sustainable than intensive agriculture.
  • After a pronoun following than, formal English uses a subject pronoun: She is older than I. (informal: than me)
  • Use in after superlatives for places: It's the best restaurant in the city.
  • Use of for groups/pronouns: She's the most sensible of the three.
  • Use among for groups: Mark is the tallest among the boys.
  • Use as … as for equality: She speaks French as fluently as a native speaker.
  • Use not so / not as … as for inferiority: This variety is not as resistant as the original.
  • That-clause is common after superlatives: She's the funniest person that I know.
💡 Adverbs: Most adverbs form comparatives with more / the most. But fast, hard, late, near, slow, soon, early add -er / -est:
Boys usually run more quickly than girls. / Elle runs faster than Becca.

6.4Comparatives & Superlatives with Nouns

+
Noun typeComparativeSuperlativeExample
Uncountablemore / less (than)the most / the leastThere is less snow this year than last. / This region gets the most rainfall.
Plural countablemore / fewer (than)the most / the fewestFewer students failed the test. / This class has the fewest absences.
❌ Less vs. Fewer — don't mix them up:
fewer cars, fewer people, fewer mistakes (countable plural ✓)
less traffic, less time, less water (uncountable ✓)
Less cars on the road ✗ → Fewer cars ✓
More Examples from the Book

There is less snow this year than last year.
Mark spent the most time revising for his oral test.
Sean did the least work of all the group.
More cycle routes mean fewer accidents.
Of all the exam papers, Jean's had the fewest mistakes.

6.5Modifiers

+

Modifiers are adverbs placed before comparatives to make the comparison stronger or weaker.

Strong Modifiers — big difference

ModifierExample
much / a lot / farThis new device is much faster than the old one. / Italian is far easier than Japanese.
evenThe second test was even harder than the first — I didn't expect that.

Weak Modifiers — small difference

ModifierExample
a bit / a little / slightlyToday is a bit warmer than yesterday. / Her accent is slightly different.
ratherThe exam was rather more difficult than we had expected.

Superlative Modifier

ModifierExample
by far (strong emphasis on the superlative)This is by far the best pasta I have ever tasted.

Any / No with Comparatives

Examples

any → in questions and negatives: Is this variety any more resistant to frost?
no → emphasises zero difference: The situation is no better than it was six months ago.

6.6Expressions Using Comparisons

+

The + comparative … the + comparative

Shows two changes that happen together — as one increases/decreases, so does the other.

Examples

The older I get, the happier I am.
The more you practise, the better you get.
The harder you study, the more confident you'll feel in the exam.
The further from the coast, the drier the climate.
The sooner you start, the more quickly you'll finish.

Comparative + and + comparative

Expresses a gradual, continuing change in one direction.

Examples

Social media is becoming more and more influential every year.
Climate change is making summers hotter and hotter.
Organic produce is getting more and more popular in Italy.
I am getting fatter and fatter.
We are going more and more slowly.

💡 Form rule: One-syllable adjectives → repeat the same form: hotter and hotter ✓. Three+ syllables → always use more and more: more and more interesting ✓. Never: more interesting and more interesting ✗.
Unit 7 · The Passive

7.1The Passive

+

When to Use the Passive

  • The agent is unknown: My phone has been stolen!
  • The agent is unimportant or obvious: Wine is produced in many Italian regions.
  • To emphasise the agent (with by): The Colosseum was built by the Romans.
  • More interest in what happened than who did it: The building was designed by an American architect.
  • Formal / scientific writing: The experiment was conducted at low temperatures.
  • When or where something happened: The match was held in summer.

All Tenses in Passive Form

TenseActivePassive (be + past participle)
Present simpleI eat an apple.An apple is eaten.
Present continuousI am eating an apple.An apple is being eaten.
Present perfectI have eaten an apple.An apple has been eaten.
Past simpleI ate an apple.An apple was eaten.
Past continuousI was eating an apple.An apple was being eaten.
Past perfectI had eaten an apple.An apple had been eaten.
Past perfect continuousI had been eating an apple.An apple had been being eaten. (rare)
Future simpleI will eat an apple.An apple will be eaten.
Future perfectI will have eaten an apple.An apple will have been eaten.

Active → Passive: The 5-Step Method

  1. The object of the active becomes the subject of the passive.
  2. Identify the tense of the main verb in the active.
  3. Form the same tense of the verb be.
  4. Add the past participle of the main verb.
  5. The active subject becomes the by-phrase — omit it if the agent is unknown or unimportant.
Step-by-step Transformations

Active: Michelangelo painted the Sistine Chapel.
Passive: The Sistine Chapel was painted by Michelangelo.

Active: They will open a new school canteen next year.
Passive: A new school canteen will be opened next year.

Active: Someone has stolen my bike.
Passive: My bike has been stolen. (agent omitted — unknown)

Active: A scientist was designing the device.
Passive: The device was being designed by a scientist.

❌ Important: Only transitive verbs (verbs that take an object) can be made passive. Intransitive verbs like arrive, happen, exist, disappear cannot be used in the passive.
💡 By vs. With: Use by for the agent (the doer): The letter was signed by the principal.
Use with for the instrument (the tool used): The letter was written with a fountain pen.
